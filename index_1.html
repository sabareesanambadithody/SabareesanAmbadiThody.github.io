About me

I am a biophysicist with over twelve years of experience studying how proteins and small molecules organize themselves inside living cells. I earned my PhD in Biological Sciences from the National Centre for Biological Sciences (Tata Institute of Fundamental Research), then completed my postdoctoral training in the Department of Biophysics/HHMI at UT Southwestern Medical Center, where I now serve as an Instructor. What drives my research is a simple question: what rules decide which molecules end up where inside a cell, and what happens when those rules break down in disease. I bring that same curiosity to applied, translational problems beyond the bench, including AI-driven diagnostics and therapeutics.

Research

How small molecules organize, and are organized by, biomolecular condensates.

My work sits at the interface of biochemistry, biophysics, and quantitative chemical biology. I build integrative frameworks (combining structural biology, biophysical characterization, metabolomics, mass-spectrometry-based proteomics, and cellular assays) to ask quantitative questions about how molecules organize inside cells.

My current work asks how small molecules (metabolites, drugs, fluorescent probes) partition into biomolecular condensates. While it was increasingly clear how proteins and nucleic acids assemble into these compartments, almost nothing was known about how small molecules find their way in. I built a high-throughput screening platform combining LC-MS, targeted and untargeted metabolomics, and fluorescence microscopy, and used it to measure partitioning coefficients for roughly 3,000 small molecules across condensates formed by diverse protein and nucleic acid scaffolds. The differences between compounds spanned nearly a million-fold, yet a machine learning model built on just a handful of computed physicochemical features (chiefly solubility and hydrophobicity) predicted partitioning with striking accuracy. Condensates, it turns out, behave as chemically distinct, largely hydrophobic microenvironments rather than sites of specific molecular recognition.

In the most recent phase of this work, I reconstituted G3BP1-induced stress-granule-like condensates in cell lysate and profiled how hundreds of endogenous metabolites and over a thousand FDA-approved drugs partition into them. About one in ten compounds showed strong, selective enrichment, evidence that even in a complex, crowded cellular environment, condensates carve out a distinct physicochemical niche that shapes which small molecules concentrate where.

Current research projects

Three active threads in the lab, spanning condensate biology and drug safety.

1. Decoding how stress granules reshape cell signaling

Using cell-free reconstitution with quantitative proteomics and metabolomics on G3BP1 stress granules, we find these condensates act as selective filters, concentrating upstream signaling kinases while excluding their regulators, and maintaining a distinct metabolite microenvironment.

Read full project

Cells organize their interior using membraneless droplets (biomolecular condensates) that concentrate specific molecules. Stress granules, which assemble around the scaffold protein G3BP1, can switch signaling pathways on or off, yet the rules governing what they capture remain unclear. Using cell-free reconstitution together with quantitative proteomics and metabolomics, we measure how strongly thousands of proteins and hundreds of metabolites partition into G3BP1 condensates. We find that partitioning is highly selective: the granule concentrates upstream signaling kinases and scaffolds while excluding their regulators and downstream effectors, and it maintains a distinct metabolite microenvironment enriched for nucleotides and messengers such as cyclic AMP. Notably, the cancer-associated kinase c-Raf enters stripped of its normal regulatory partners.

These findings support a working model of the stress granule as a selective molecular filter that co-organizes proteins and metabolites to remodel signaling. We are now extending this work into living cells to explore how condensate-based sequestration influences stress adaptation and drug responses in cancer.

2. What makes a protein want to join a stress granule?

Working with partitioning data for roughly 1,300 proteins and 240 metabolites, we're testing what physical properties predict enrichment in stress granules, and finding that electrostatic charge, especially within disordered regions, is a promising but still partial answer.

Read full project

Stress granules are membraneless droplets that cells assemble under stress, built around a core protein called G3BP1. Some proteins and metabolites partition strongly into these condensates; others barely enter at all, and we're investigating what physical properties predict that behavior. Working with partitioning data for roughly 1,300 human proteins and 240 metabolites, we calculated each molecule's net electrostatic charge at physiological pH and found a real but modest relationship: more positively charged proteins tend to partition more strongly into the granule, while for metabolites the relationship runs in the opposite direction, with negatively charged compounds showing higher enrichment. Neither effect explains more than a small fraction of the variation, but both are statistically robust across multiple ways of testing them.

We've also ruled out several other candidate explanations, at least in their simplest forms. Bulk hydrophobicity, protein length, being a known direct G3BP1 interactor or RNA-binding protein, and computationally predicted phase-separation propensity all showed little to no relationship with measured partitioning. The most promising lead so far is that charge localized specifically within a protein's intrinsically disordered regions may track with enrichment more closely than whole-protein charge, an early, exploratory finding that points toward our next round of analysis focused on disordered-region composition and specific charged motifs rather than bulk sequence properties alone.

3. Biomolecular condensates as a lens on drug toxicity

Does a compound's tendency to partition into biomolecular condensates carry information about its toxicity risk? Using partitioning data across eight condensate systems and machine learning, we find chemical signatures that recur across thousands of approved, withdrawn, and investigational drugs.

Read full project

Our lab is exploring a question at the intersection of cell biology and drug safety: does a small molecule's tendency to partition into biomolecular condensates (the membraneless, phase-separated compartments increasingly recognized as central hubs of cellular organization) carry information about its potential to cause organ toxicity? Using mass spectrometry-based partitioning measurements across eight reconstituted condensate systems and a validated machine learning pipeline, we've shown that this partitioning behavior is predictable from chemical structure, and that specific chemical signatures (aromaticity linked to hepatotoxicity, flexible basic-nitrogen-containing scaffolds linked to cardiotoxicity) recur consistently across thousands of approved, withdrawn, and investigational compounds drawn from independent pharmaceutical libraries.

The emerging picture is more nuanced than a simple "condensates cause toxicity" story: our evidence to date is most consistent with condensate partitioning and certain toxicities sharing common upstream chemical drivers (lipophilicity, molecular planarity) rather than one directly causing the other, and we're currently designing confound-controlled experiments (including direct condensate-disruption assays in relevant cell systems) to test this distinction rigorously. We see this as a genuine opportunity to add a mechanistically grounded, complementary layer to existing computational toxicology tools, and we're actively pursuing wet-lab validation to determine exactly where this approach adds real predictive value.
